\chapter*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

The disagreement between materialism and idealism is commonly framed as a dispute over what reality is fundamentally made of, yet both positions begin inside descriptive grammars that have already decided what counts as an object, a state, a cause, an interior, an exterior, and a direction of explanation. This work therefore begins one level earlier. It asks which languages preserve the distinctions reality requires us to preserve, which distinctions a chosen formalism collapses, which directions a chosen observation algebra cannot recover, and only then what ontology the surviving evidence permits us to affirm. Mathematics is treated neither as an infallible mirror of reality nor as a merely convenient notation, but as a family of formal languages whose state spaces, transformations, metrics, quotients, symmetry groups, dynamical laws, probability structures, and invariant algebras determine what can be resolved, what is identified, and what orientation remains invisible.

The book develops two primary failure modes of description. A language can lose \emph{resolution}: genuinely different states may be identified because the available invariants are too coarse. A language can also lose \emph{orientation}: states may share every measured coarse-form descriptor while differing in directed relational structure. These two failures are mathematically distinct and become the organizing axis of the audit. The first is exposed by invariant theory; the second by wave conjugation. Together they motivate a broader governing rule: observed relation does not by itself determine direction of predication.

The mathematical spine begins with a relational state and separates state identity from form identity. Two reduced states may be genuinely different while instantiating the same form, so persistence need not require stasis. Coupled oscillators and Stuart--Landau systems separate collective motion, relational deformation, and amplitude change. Finite-group projectors on the sphere provide exact measures of Platonic symmetry coherence, while character theory separates mathematical admissibility from nonlinear selection. The symmetry language is explicitly classical: the analysis concerns the Platonic rotation groups, their invariant subspaces, dualities, and higher-order invariant extensions; it does not posit a separate family of ``neo-Platonic solids.''

In the real $\ell=5$ representation of $SO(3)$, the quartic reduced energy possesses an exact five-dimensional local minimum manifold at an explicit regular point. Three tangent directions are rotational and two are genuine shape-flat directions. Sixth-order invariant structure contains four genuinely new invariant directions beyond lower-order products, and an explicit sextic invariant has positive-definite intrinsic Hessian on both unresolved quartic shape directions. Hence a richer invariant algebra distinguishes physical structure unavailable to the lower-order description. This yields the descriptive-refinement principle: indistinguishability under a restricted descriptive algebra does not imply identity under every richer descriptive algebra.

Complex wave conjugation supplies a complementary theorem of orientation. For $\Psi=Ae^{i\theta}$, conjugation preserves norm, density, finite-group symmetry coherence, and stabilizer while reversing the standard directed current,

\[
\mathbf j_{\Psi^*}=-\mathbf j_\Psi.
\]

Thus invariant appearance need not determine directed relational structure. Platonic duality and complex conjugation are distinct in general, and a fixed geometry can support inequivalent dynamical laws, so form, duality, process, and direction cannot be collapsed into one mathematical vocabulary. The result is not a derivation of idealism or consciousness-first ontology. Its role is methodological: it demonstrates exactly that a descriptive language can preserve coarse form while failing to determine orientation.

This orientation result motivates an audit of the arrow hidden inside ordinary language. Words such as \emph{produces}, \emph{emerges from}, \emph{appears within}, \emph{manifests}, \emph{projects}, and \emph{constitutes} encode directed claims even when the nouns on either side remain unchanged. The physical--experiential problem is therefore formulated as a competition among directed model families rather than as a debate whose arrow has been fixed in advance:

\[
\mathcal M_P:\quad P\longrightarrow E,
\qquad
\mathcal M_E:\quad E\longrightarrow P,
\qquad
\mathcal M_R:\quad P\longleftarrow R\longrightarrow E.
\]

The observational joint distribution does not by itself determine which directed structure is causal or ontologically prior. Physical intervention genuinely constrains every viable model, but experimental accessibility and ontological priority are different propositions. The direction of predication must therefore be earned through intervention, temporal and dynamical structure, conditional independence, cross-context invariance, closure, predictive transfer, and complexity-controlled model comparison.

Four object-level boundaries govern the resulting architecture. Restricted descriptive indistinguishability is not identity under every richer description. Invariant observational equivalence is not identity of directed relational structure. Descriptive adequacy is not ontological sufficiency. Mathematical admissibility is not phenomenal occupancy. Above them sits the cross-cutting Direction-of-Predication Principle: relation alone does not license an arrow. The first two boundaries audit mathematical language; the third governs the passage from description to ontology; the fourth governs the passage from formal consciousness models to claims about actual experience; the governing principle prevents any of those relations from acquiring causal or ontological direction merely through grammar or convention.

Consciousness is consequently treated as the hardest application of the language audit rather than as the premise from which the mathematics begins. Observable phenomenological evidence is represented by a latent variable $Z_\varphi$ that is explicitly not identified with experience itself. An ontology earns the description of consciousness only by moving through a non-implicational evidential sequence: formal admissibility, structural nonredundancy, predictive necessity, interventional necessity, cross-context invariance, independent phenomenological anchoring, best-supported occupancy modeling, and only then ontological interpretation. Linguistic report, neural correlation, integration, self-modeling, dimensional complementarity, or an unexplained informational residual cannot be promoted directly into phenomenality.

The empirical program remains anchored to one primary experimental arena rather than expanding into an unconstrained menu of studies. Graded anesthesia and recovery, combined with perturbational neurophysiology, dense physical measurement, and independently constrained phenomenological evidence, provide a testbed for comparing predication structures under one protocol. Increasingly comprehensive physical descriptions are tested for screening and approximate closure; relational models are tested for nonredundant common structure; physical interventions test directed dependence; transfer and conditional-independence relations test invariance; and capacity-matched alternatives prevent a richer model from winning merely because it contains more degrees of freedom. The program is explicitly defeasible: physical absorption, bridge instability, failure of transfer, intervention failure, capacity equivalence, or non-specific transformation laws can each defeat the corresponding stronger interpretation.

The resulting question is broader than the classical mind--body problem. Platonic symmetry asks what remains. Higher-order invariant theory asks what a language can distinguish. Wave dynamics asks how realization changes. Conjugation asks whether direction can reverse while invariant form remains. Plain language asks which distinctions and arrows its grammar silently assumes. Causal modeling asks which direction survives intervention. Ontology asks what, after all of those audits, reality has actually earned the right to be called. The central aim is therefore not to force matter, mind, form, process, or relation into premature primacy, but to construct a hierarchy of descriptive and empirical tests stringent enough that no ontology wins merely because its preferred arrow was drawn first.
