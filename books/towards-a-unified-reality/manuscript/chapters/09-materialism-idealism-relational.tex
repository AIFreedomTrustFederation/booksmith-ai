\chapter{Materialism, Idealism, and Relational Ontology}

\section{The Direction of Predication}

Every ontology of consciousness arrives with an arrow, whether it acknowledges that arrow or hides it inside its vocabulary. The conventional grammar is

\[
P \longrightarrow E,
\]

where $P$ denotes the relevant physical-relational state and $E$ denotes experiential structure. Physical organization is treated as prior; experience appears downstream.

But this direction is not contained in the bare fact that physical and experiential states covary. At the level of observational factorization,

\[
p(P,E)
=
p(P)\,p(E\mid P)
=
p(E)\,p(P\mid E).
\]

The same joint distribution can therefore be written in either direction. Probability alone does not tell us which factorization represents causal predication.

This opens three conceptually distinct model families:

\[
\mathcal M_P:
\qquad
P\longrightarrow E,
\]

\[
p(P,E)
=
p(P)\,p(E\mid P),
\]

representing physical-first predication;

\[
\mathcal M_E:
\qquad
E\longrightarrow P,
\]

\[
p(P,E)
=
p(E)\,p(P\mid E),
\]

representing experiential-first predication; and

\[
\mathcal M_R:
\qquad
P\longleftarrow R\longrightarrow E,
\]

\[
p(P,E,R)
=
p(R)\,p(P\mid R)\,p(E\mid R),
\]

representing a common relational source from which physical exteriority and experiential interiority are separately projected.

These factorizations must not be mistaken for proofs of the corresponding ontologies. The first two probability factorizations are mathematically available for any ordinary joint distribution. Their ontological significance appears only when causal, temporal, interventional, and independence structure is added.

The question is therefore not merely whether $P$ predicts $E$, because prediction can run in either statistical direction. The question is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Which directed structure survives intervention?}
}
\]

That is the point at which predication becomes an empirical problem.

\subsection{What Intervention Can Actually Establish}

Suppose an intervention alters a physical variable $P_i$ while appropriately controlling competing pathways, and an experiential indicator changes:

\[
p(E\mid \operatorname{do}(P_i=p_1))
\neq
p(E\mid \operatorname{do}(P_i=p_2)).
\]

This is genuine evidence that the manipulated physical variable lies on a causal route relevant to experience.

Anesthesia, pharmacological perturbation, electrical or magnetic stimulation, lesions, and related manipulations therefore matter profoundly. They establish that changing physical organization can change conditions associated with conscious experience.

But the inference must stop exactly where the intervention stops.

The result

\[
\operatorname{do}(P_i)\Rightarrow \Delta E
\]

does not by itself establish the complete ontological proposition

\[
P_{\mathrm{complete}}\longrightarrow E.
\]

It establishes a causal dependence involving the manipulated physical variable.

Likewise, such an intervention does constrain a simple one-way model

\[
E\longrightarrow P
\]

in which $P$ is purely downstream and has no return path to $E$. A consciousness-first ontology therefore cannot simply ignore physical intervention results. It must explain them.

It could posit reciprocal causation,

\[
E\rightleftarrows P,
\]

or embed both variables in a deeper relational dynamics,

\[
P\longleftarrow R\longrightarrow E,
\]

possibly with additional feedback,

\[
P\rightleftarrows R\rightleftarrows E.
\]

Or it could propose that what neuroscience labels $P$ is itself already one projection of a deeper structure rather than an ontologically independent cause.

Those are different theories and must make different predictions.

The important point is therefore more precise than saying that physical intervention cannot favor one ontology over another:

\[
\boxed{
\text{physical intervention constrains every viable ontology,}
\quad
\text{but does not automatically establish physical ontological priority.}
}
\]

A demonstrated arrow through a physical variable is not yet a theorem that all arrows originate in the physical.

\subsection{The Asymmetry of Experimental Access}

There is nevertheless a serious experimental asymmetry.

Physical variables can be manipulated through physical procedures. We can alter receptor binding, membrane potentials, network dynamics, sensory input, metabolism, cortical excitability, and many other measurable variables.

An alleged experiential variable cannot presently be isolated and manipulated independently of every physical realization in anything like the same straightforward manner.

That asymmetry limits causal identification.

It does not license the inference

\[
\text{only physically accessible interventions exist}
\quad\therefore\quad
\text{only physical causation exists}.
\]

Experimental accessibility and ontological priority are different propositions.

The first concerns what our intervention technology can manipulate.

The second concerns what reality fundamentally contains.

Confusing them would violate the same descriptive discipline developed earlier in this book: a limitation of the available measurement or intervention language must not be promoted into a limitation of reality itself.

\subsection{Conditional Independence as a Second Discriminator}

The competing models also differ in their proposed screening relations.

For a simple relational common-cause model,

\[
P\longleftarrow R\longrightarrow E,
\]

one expects, under the model's assumptions,

\[
P\perp E\mid R.
\]

Once the relevant relational source is completely specified, the residual dependence between its two projections should disappear.

A strong physical-sufficiency model instead seeks some sufficiently complete physical state $P^\ast$ such that

\[
E\perp R_{\mathrm{extra}}\mid P^\ast
\]

for any proposed additional variable $R_{\mathrm{extra}}$, while the physical description is also dynamically autonomous:

\[
\dot P^\ast=f(P^\ast).
\]

If every experiential distinction can be reconstructed from $P^\ast$, every additional candidate variable becomes redundant, and the physical dynamics close upon themselves, then the physical-first account has earned substantially more than a mere correlation.

Conversely, if two deeper states share the same proposed complete physical description,

\[
P^\ast(r_1)=P^\ast(r_2),
\]

while producing different empirically warranted experiential consequences,

\[
E(r_1)\neq E(r_2),
\]

then the proposed $P^\ast$ was not sufficient.

That would not automatically prove experiential-first ontology.

It would prove something more modest and more important:

\[
\boxed{
\text{the tested physical description was incomplete for the distinction at issue.}
}
\]

This is exactly the logic already encountered in the invariant audit.

\subsection{Why Wave Conjugation Belongs Here}

The mathematical image motivating the reversal of predication comes from complex conjugation.

Let

\[
\Psi=Ae^{i\theta}.
\]

Its conjugate is

\[
K\Psi
=
\Psi^\ast
=
Ae^{-i\theta}.
\]

Under the conditions developed earlier in this work,

\[
|\Psi^\ast|^2
=
|\Psi|^2,
\]

\[
C_G(\Psi^\ast)
=
C_G(\Psi),
\]

and

\[
\operatorname{Stab}(\Psi^\ast)
=
\operatorname{Stab}(\Psi).
\]

The coarse invariant form is therefore preserved.

Yet the directed current obeys

\[
\boxed{
\mathbf j_{\Psi^\ast}
=
-\mathbf j_\Psi.
}
\]

The same invariant appearance admits opposite directed flow.

This is not a derivation of idealism.

It does not imply

\[
P\rightarrow E
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
E\rightarrow P.
\]

Nor does complex conjugation transform matter into consciousness.

Its significance is methodological.

It provides an exact mathematical demonstration that \emph{invariant form does not necessarily determine directional orientation}.

Hence

\[
\boxed{
\text{invariant observational equivalence}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{identity of directed relational structure}.
}
\]

That proposition belongs beside the descriptive-refinement theorem obtained from the quartic/sextic audit:

\[
\boxed{
\text{lower-resolution indistinguishability}
\not\Rightarrow
\text{higher-resolution identity}.
}
\]

The two results identify different failures of description.

The quartic/sextic result exposes a failure of \emph{resolution}.

Wave conjugation exposes a potential failure of \emph{orientation}.

A descriptive language can therefore erase distinctions in at least two fundamentally different ways:

\[
\boxed{
\text{it can fail to resolve what differs,}
\quad\text{or fail to orient what runs oppositely.}
}
\]

\subsection{The Arrow Hidden Inside Ordinary Language}

This becomes especially important when mathematics is translated into ordinary philosophical language.

Consider the sentences:

\begin{quote}
Consciousness emerges from physical reality.
\end{quote}

and

\begin{quote}
Physical reality appears within consciousness.
\end{quote}

The nouns attract most of the philosophical dispute: \emph{consciousness}, \emph{matter}, \emph{physical}, \emph{experience}.

Yet the deepest disagreement may be carried by something linguistically much smaller:

\[
\boxed{\longrightarrow}
\]

The direction of predication differs.

One description says

\[
P\rightarrow E.
\]

The other says

\[
E\rightarrow P.
\]

A third refuses both privileged directions:

\[
P\leftarrow R\rightarrow E.
\]

The language audit must therefore examine verbs, dependency, and direction just as carefully as nouns.

Words such as \emph{produces}, \emph{emerges from}, \emph{generates}, \emph{manifests}, \emph{appears within}, \emph{projects}, and \emph{constitutes} are not stylistic ornaments. They encode causal or ontological arrows.

An ontology can therefore smuggle its conclusion into ordinary grammar before evidence has ever been examined.

The sentence

\begin{quote}
the brain produces consciousness
\end{quote}

already contains more than the empirical statement

\begin{quote}
changing some brain states changes some reliable indicators associated with consciousness.
\end{quote}

The first asserts an ontological direction.

The second reports a causal result.

The distance between those two sentences is precisely the territory this audit is designed to protect.

\subsection{Predication as a Model-Selection Problem}

The three broad possibilities should therefore remain distinct:

\[
\mathcal M_P:
\qquad
P\rightarrow E,
\]

\[
\mathcal M_E:
\qquad
E\rightarrow P,
\]

\[
\mathcal M_R:
\qquad
P\leftarrow R\rightarrow E.
\]

More elaborate models may include feedback, temporal recursion, latent variables, and different scales of description. The three structures above are therefore canonical starting families, not an exhaustive catalog of all possible ontologies.

Their competition cannot be settled by prose.

It requires evidence capable of distinguishing directed models: intervention, temporal precedence where meaningful, conditional independence, invariance across contexts, perturbation, predictive transfer, dynamical closure, and complexity-controlled comparison.

The physical-first model must earn the claim that an appropriately complete physical-relational state is sufficient and autonomous.

The experiential-first model must earn the claim that experiential structure has genuine predictive or causal priority that cannot be reproduced by physical or common-cause alternatives.

The relational model must earn the existence and necessity of a common structure $R$, rather than using $R$ merely as a label for whatever remains unexplained.

Every model therefore has something substantial to lose.

\subsection{The Direction-of-Predication Principle}

The audit leads to a general methodological principle:

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{Direction of Predication Principle}
}
\]

If multiple directed models reproduce the same observational structure, the direction of ontological predication is not licensed by observational equivalence alone. It must be earned through additional causal, interventional, dynamical, or independence structure.

Equivalently,

\[
\boxed{
p(P,E)
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
P\rightarrow E,
}
\]

\[
\boxed{
p(P,E)
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
E\rightarrow P,
}
\]

and

\[
\boxed{
p(P,E)
\;\not\Rightarrow\;
P\leftarrow R\rightarrow E.
}
\]

The distribution gives the relation.

It does not, by itself, give the direction or ontology of that relation.

This principle returns us directly to the larger thesis of the book.

Platonic symmetry asks:

\begin{quote}
What remains invariant?
\end{quote}

The quartic/sextic audit asks:

\begin{quote}
At what descriptive resolution can a difference be seen?
\end{quote}

Wave dynamics asks:

\begin{quote}
How does the state transform?
\end{quote}

Conjugation asks:

\begin{quote}
Can directional structure reverse while invariant form remains?
\end{quote}

Plain language asks:

\begin{quote}
Which distinctions and directions have our words preserved, collapsed, or silently assumed?
\end{quote}

And ontology finally asks:

\begin{quote}
Which of those descriptions has earned the right to say not merely how reality can be represented, but how reality actually predicates itself?
\end{quote}

The answer cannot be read off from the arrow we inherited.

The arrow itself is part of what must be tested.

\section{The Strong Physicalist Claim}

Once descriptive adequacy has been separated from ontology, the materialism--idealism dispute can be stated more precisely. The strongest form of physicalism relevant here is not a doctrine of tiny solid objects. It is a sufficiency claim. Let $P_{\mathrm{phys},\le t}$ denote the complete public physical history required by the theory and let $E_t$ denote the experiential state. Strong physical supervenience asserts

\[
P_{\mathrm{phys},\le t}^{(1)}=P_{\mathrm{phys},\le t}^{(2)}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
E_t^{(1)}=E_t^{(2)}.
\]

No two systems can be physically identical in the complete relevant sense and experientially different. The corresponding causal-closure claim can be written schematically as

\[
p(Y_{t+\Delta}\mid P_{\mathrm{phys},\le t},E_t,do(A_t))
=
p(Y_{t+\Delta}\mid P_{\mathrm{phys},\le t},do(A_t)).
\]

Once the complete physical state is supplied, adding experience contributes no further information to physical prediction.

This formulation matters because it prevents the present mathematics from attacking a caricature. Relational states, complex waves, field descriptions, higher-order invariants, nonlocal mathematical representations, and dynamic symmetry breaking can all be accepted by a sophisticated physicalist. The physicalist need only maintain that these structures, whatever their final mathematical form, are the complete physical reality and are sufficient for experience. Wave ontology is therefore not idealism, and relational mathematics does not defeat physicalism merely by replacing object language with process language.

Naive substance materialism is more directly pressured. If persistent identity can be represented through relational organization even while components change, then immutable material bearers are not required for every form of identity. But this is a reorganization within possible physical ontology, not a proof that physical ontology fails.

\section{The Idealist Alternative}

Idealism assigns explanatory priority to mind, experience, intelligibility, or form. A relational idealist formulation can begin from a fundamental experiential-relational state $\Xi$ with dynamics

\[
\dot\Xi=G_I(\Xi),
\]

and treat the public physical world as a projection

\[
P_{\mathrm{phys},t}=\Pi(\Xi_{\le t}).
\]

The mathematical work in earlier chapters is compatible with such a view because invariant form can organize many changing realizations, but compatibility is not evidence. The fact that mathematical form plays a central role in classification does not establish that forms exist independently of physical realization. The fact that consciousness is epistemically immediate does not by itself determine the generative direction between experience and physical structure.

The Platonic material in this book should therefore be read carefully. Platonic symmetry offers an exact mathematical language of invariant form. It does not prove Platonic idealism. The affinity is philosophical: both emphasize structure that persists through changing appearance. The derivation itself remains neutral about whether the invariant is instantiated physically, mentally, or within some deeper ontology.

\section{Relational Dual-Aspect Realism}

A third possibility treats physical exteriority and experiential interiority as two potentially irreducible aspects of one deeper relational actuality. Let $r\in\mathscr R$ and define

\[
P_{\mathrm{phys}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r),
\qquad
E_{\mathrm{exp}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{int}}(r).
\]

Schematically,

\[
P_{\mathrm{phys}}\longleftarrow \mathscr R\longrightarrow E_{\mathrm{exp}}.
\]

This is not intended as substance dualism. The model contains one underlying relational state, not two independently interacting substances. Yet the diagram by itself is almost vacuous. Given sufficiently large latent space, one can always define a common container that stores two sets of variables. A scientifically meaningful dual-aspect model must therefore impose a nontrivial structural restriction on $\mathscr R$ and derive an observable consequence that cannot be reproduced equally well by simpler single-aspect models.

A local rank condition provides a minimal formal notion of complementarity. If $n=\dim\mathscr R$ and

\[
\operatorname{rank}D\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}<n,
\qquad
\operatorname{rank}D\Pi_{\mathrm{int}}<n,
\]

neither projection locally reconstructs the underlying state. If the joint map satisfies

\[
\operatorname{rank}D(\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}},\Pi_{\mathrm{int}})=n,
\]

then the pair is locally sufficient even though each projection is individually incomplete.

This is a genuine mathematical notion of complementarity, but its semantic weakness must be stated immediately rather than after the machinery has acquired rhetorical momentum. Local rank complementarity establishes complementary coordinates or projections. It does not establish that one projection is phenomenal interiority. The interpretation of $E_{\mathrm{exp}}$ requires independent phenomenological anchoring.

\section{Dynamical Closure and Its Ambiguity}

Suppose the underlying state evolves by

\[
\dot r=F(r).
\]

The exterior projection obeys

\[
\dot P_{\mathrm{phys}}=D\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r)F(r).
\]

For the projected physical state to possess a closed autonomous dynamics, there must exist $f$ such that

\[
\dot P_{\mathrm{phys}}=f(P_{\mathrm{phys}}).
\]

If two underlying states satisfy

\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r_1)=\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r_2)
\]

but

\[
D\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r_1)F(r_1)\neq D\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}(r_2)F(r_2),
\]

then no exact autonomous law on that projection exists. A dual-aspect realization could in principle exhibit nonclosure of $P$ and nonclosure of $E$ separately while the pair $(P,E)$ closes.

The inference remains ambiguous because an incomplete physical coarse-graining produces the same mathematical symptom. Imagine a physical system with hidden phase $\theta$ and measured variable $x$. If $\dot x$ depends on $\theta$, then two states with the same $x$ but different hidden phase have different derivatives. The measured $x$ does not close, yet nothing nonphysical has been introduced. The failure simply tells us that the chosen physical description was incomplete.

This counterexample is central to the entire ontology audit. A failure of measured physical closure cannot be promoted directly into evidence against physicalism. The physical state must be enriched, alternative hidden physical variables must be tested, and approximate rather than exact closure must be considered because real experiments rarely recover every fast and slow variable exactly. The empirical program in Chapter 11 is designed around this problem.

\section{Difference Without Absolute Separation}

The relational framework also clarifies a philosophical confusion that often enters discussions of duality. Mathematical difference does not imply absolute ontological separation. In symbols, $A\neq B$ does not entail that $A$ and $B$ belong to unrelated substances or disconnected domains. Two projections may differ because they preserve different relational information. A state and its conjugate may differ while sharing density and symmetry. Two coordinates may differ while belonging to one underlying manifold. None of these examples proves dual-aspect monism, but all show that difference alone does not force substance dualism.

The ontological contest therefore remains genuinely open. Relational physicalism says the complete physical relational description is sufficient. Relational idealism assigns priority to experiential or intelligible relation. Relational dual-aspect realism proposes that one deeper actuality supports irreducible exterior and interior projections. The next chapters ask what evidence could discriminate among these claims without allowing the mathematics to decide by naming convention.
