# Chapter 1: Why Energence?

## The Governing Question

Before we ask whether God exists, we should ask what we mean when we say **God**.

The word arrives before most of us are old enough to examine it. It may arrive clothed in the voice of a father, the tenderness of a mother, the authority of a preacher, the fear of punishment, the silence of grief, or the wonder of a night sky. Long before God becomes a philosophical question, God becomes an image within us. For some, that image becomes a refuge. For others, it becomes a wound. For many, it becomes both.

The inherited image is often that of a magnified human ruler: an invisible masculine person who exists somewhere beyond the universe, intervenes selectively within it, issues commands, rewards obedience, and permits catastrophes that no loving human parent would willingly allow. This image has carried genuine faith, courage, repentance, and hope for generations. It has also produced an intellectual and spiritual fracture. The more completely nature is explained through physical processes, the less work such a God appears to perform. The more closely suffering is examined, the harder it becomes to reconcile arbitrary intervention with divine goodness. The more deeply human cultures and scriptures are studied, the more clearly every description of God bears the language, symbols, fears, and hopes of the people speaking.

Many modern people therefore feel forced into a choice: preserve God by denying what science reveals, or preserve intellectual integrity by abandoning God altogether.

This book refuses that choice.

It begins from a different possibility: perhaps the conflict persists because both sides inherited too small a meaning of God. Perhaps divinity is not one object among other objects, one cause competing with natural causes, or one supernatural mind occasionally reaching into an otherwise godless machine. Perhaps God is the inexhaustible reality within which nature exists, the power by which it becomes, the intelligibility through which it takes form, and the transcendent depth that no single form can contain.

I call this reality **Energence**.

The name joins two realities that can be distinguished but never finally separated: **Energy** and **Intelligence**. Energy names the capacity for action, transformation, movement, and causal consequence. Intelligence, at its most primordial level, does not yet mean a humanlike mind thinking private thoughts. It means the information-bearing and formative capacity through which possibility enters relationship, relationship acquires constraint, and constraint permits coherent form. Energy without differentiation would accomplish nothing describable. Information without energetic actuality would remain an abstraction incapable of becoming a universe. Existence appears not as naked power or disembodied plan, but as power continually assuming intelligible relations.

Energence names their uncreated unity.

This is a theological interpretation, not a laboratory measurement. Physics can describe energy, states, symmetries, fields, interactions, and information without naming any of them divine. Biology can describe heredity, variation, selection, development, regulation, and emergence without invoking sacred intention. Neuroscience can investigate perception and consciousness without deciding the ultimate metaphysical meaning of mind. This book will not smuggle God into scientific vocabulary and then claim that a change of names amounts to proof.

The question is deeper. Once science has described, with increasing precision, how reality behaves, what kind of reality has science described? Why is there a universe capable of stable relationship, lawful transformation, self-organization, living memory, awareness, moral struggle, and conscious inquiry? Are these merely disconnected accidents within an ultimately unintelligible fact? Or may they be understood as expressions of a single divine depth—without denying contingency, waste, suffering, or the integrity of natural explanation?

Energence is the name I give to that depth.

The word matters because names govern attention. A name does not capture God, but it can expose what an inherited name has concealed. When the divine is named only as Father, King, or Lord, masculine and political metaphors can quietly harden into metaphysical boundaries. God begins to resemble the social order that speaks in God’s name. Yet the source of all existence cannot be biologically male. If sexuality, maternity, paternity, embodiment, and consciousness arise within the divine reality, then no single creaturely gender can contain their source.

Energence is therefore gender-transcendent—not genderless in the sense of being barren or abstract, but infinitely generative beyond the division of male and female. Father-language can still reveal something true: origin, gift, ground, and generative possibility. Mother-language can reveal something equally true: formation, gestation, nurture, adaptation, and birth. Christic language reveals embodied consciousness awakening to its origin and freely participating in divine communion. None of these modes exhausts Energence. Together they disclose a movement from possibility, through formation, into conscious participation.

This movement also changes how we understand evolution.

Evolution is often presented as the final humiliation of religious belief. Life varies without foresight. Environments select without mercy. Species emerge and vanish. Most creatures that have lived are extinct. The human body bears the evidence of ancestry rather than isolated manufacture. If creation means that every organism was separately designed in its present form, evolution is indeed a threat.

But that is not the only possible meaning of creation.

What if creation is not merely an event behind the universe but the continuing act of the universe becoming? What if divine immanence is not an occasional interruption of nature but the sustaining depth of every natural process? What if the long emergence of matter, life, memory, consciousness, and ethical responsibility belongs to the history of creation itself?

In the Energence hypothesis, evolution does not replace creation. Evolution is the immanent mechanism through which creation becomes historically actual. This does not mean that every evolutionary outcome is morally good, that survival demonstrates divine favor, or that nature advances through a simple ladder of inevitable progress. Natural selection measures differential reproduction, not righteousness. Extinction is real. Suffering is real. Contingency is real. Any theology that calls evolution divine must face these realities without disguising them.

Nor does Energence claim that every particle secretly possesses a miniature human consciousness. Participation is not identity of degree. Every physical state participates in the energetic and informational relations of existence, but reflective awareness requires forms of organization that not every state possesses. The stone exists within Energence; the living cell organizes and preserves itself within Energence; the animal experiences a world within Energence; the human being can become conscious of participation itself. The difference matters. Panentheism does not flatten creation into sameness. It places every difference within a more comprehensive unity.

That unity is expressed in an ancient scriptural grammar: in God we live and move and have our being; from God, through God, and toward God are all things. These declarations do not offer a modern theory of cosmology. They do something more fundamental. They deny that creation is ever truly outside its source. They picture existence not as an artifact abandoned by a distant maker, but as a reality continuously dependent upon, surrounded by, and directed toward divine life.

The governing question of this book can therefore be stated plainly:

> Can the God encountered through Scripture, nature, evolution, consciousness, suffering, and resurrection be understood as the gender-transcendent unity of Energy and Intelligence—immanent within all becoming, yet transcending every form that has become?

I am not offering the word *Energence* as a verbal solution to every mystery. Renaming mystery does not explain it. I offer the name as an organizing hypothesis: a way to bring scientific integrity, scriptural participation, evolutionary history, and transformative faith into one sustained conversation. Its value will depend on whether it illuminates more than it obscures; whether it can face the strongest objections rather than hiding from them; whether it honors evidence without reducing reality to measurement; and whether it produces an ethic of humility, communion, responsibility, and love.

For if all things exist within Energence, no being is metaphysically disposable. If consciousness is awakened participation rather than isolated possession, then knowledge carries responsibility. If evolution has brought forth creatures capable of reflecting upon the conditions of life, then humanity is not crowned with permission to dominate but burdened with the duty to participate consciously in the healing of relationship.

The question “Why Energence?” is therefore not finally a question about terminology. It is a question about what kind of universe we inhabit, what kind of God we can truthfully name, and what kind of people we must become if the life within us is continuous with the life of the whole.

The name is new. The intuition is ancient. We do not stand outside the divine asking whether God can be found. We awaken within the mystery that has always carried us.
